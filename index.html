Three-City Public Evidence Board

Begin with what the public record supports. Explanation comes later.

City/region Documented transition Institutional response worth inspecting Claim ceiling
Youngstown Steel collapse produced major job and population loss. The Youngstown 2010 plan explicitly accepted that the city had become smaller and sought a sustainable mid-sized future. Planning shifted from an inherited growth model toward managing a smaller city and redefining its role in a regional economy. This does not prove which single policy caused later outcomes.
Pittsburgh The region suffered severe steel/manufacturing adjustment, then became less manufacturing-specialized and more service-driven, with strong healthcare and education sectors. Universities, healthcare, management, energy, and technology became more important parts of the regional economy. Diversification is a pattern, not proof that one institution “saved” the city.
Cleveland Manufacturing shocks were followed by weaker employment/population growth than many peers, while the region also developed major anchor-institution and community-wealth initiatives. Greater University Circle linked hospitals/universities, local procurement, neighborhood investment, and worker-owned Evergreen businesses. Interesting repair experiments do not imply that Cleveland solved post-industrial inequality or decline.
Public-source orientation: Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland retrospectives on Pittsburgh and Cleveland; Cleveland Fed 2026 manufacturing-employment brief; City of Youngstown 2010 planning documents; Cleveland Foundation Greater University Circle/Evergreen case materials. These sources support comparison, not a controlled causal experiment.

The Ethical Telescope

Zoom outward without losing the person at the center. Then zoom back in.

Level 1 — Person / household What happens to a worker, family, or resident when a major employer contracts, closes, relocates, or restructures?
Level 2 — Workplace / organization What roles, incentives, ownership arrangements, and formal procedures shape what managers, workers, unions, lenders, or boards can do?
Level 3 — City institutional ecology How do local government, employers, schools, hospitals, universities, unions, nonprofits, developers, and residents interact?
Level 4 — Regional / national / historical What larger forces—technology, trade, capital mobility, migration, national policy, energy, finance—change the local field of action?
Telescopic firewall: Seeing a larger structure does not erase local agency. Seeing a local decision does not exhaust the structure.

Two Levels of Repair

This comes directly from the expanded Chapter 10: change conditions and support persons. Often both are necessary.

Level A — Change conditions

Change a rule, schedule, reporting route, ownership/investment mechanism, procurement practice, planning process, incentive, or decision structure.

Level B — Support persons

Training, education, mentoring, mobility support, counseling, skill development, rest, or other capacity-building may still be necessary.
